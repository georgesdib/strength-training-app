# Upper-body throws

**Status:** implemented 2026-10-31 (`DEC-POWER-05`).

## Current prescription

| Session | Exercise | Dose | Stop rule |
|---|---|---|---|
| Bench + squat | Medicine-Ball Chest Pass | 3 × 3–5 | Maximal intent; full recovery; stop when pass speed or quality drops |
| Bench + hinge | Medicine-Ball Chest Pass | 3 × 3–5 | Maximal intent; full recovery; stop when pass speed or quality drops |

The throw is first in the session, before the bench main lift. It is implement-only: the engine records 0 kg, no e1RM and no double progression. The athlete chooses a light ball; quality is the progression.

## Why a chest pass

- It is a horizontal upper-body power expression, complementary to the vertical box jump and horizontal broad jump.
- It has low axial spinal loading compared with loaded jumps or Olympic-style lifts.
- It is simple to dose by quality and does not require a rack or platform.

## Evidence

There is no direct trial of medicine-ball chest passes improving bench-press 1RM. The evidence is indirect:

- power training improves power and physical function, with low-to-moderate certainty; fast concentric work and low-to-moderate volume are supported ([ACSM overview of reviews, 2026](https://doi.org/10.1249/MSS.0000000000003897));
- upper-body plyometric/throw training is widely used as a power stimulus, but modality-specific and bench-specific evidence is sparse;
- the exact 3 × 3–5 dose is practice, not a validated protocol.

**Classification:** general power practice, not a validated bench-specific intervention.

## Boundaries

- One throw slot per bench session; no second throw or jump in the same session.
- No prescribed medicine-ball mass, no RIR, no e1RM and no double progression.
- If no medicine ball or suitable wall/partner is available, skip the slot with a visible note; do not silently substitute another exercise.
- No overhead or rotational throw is added in this change.
- Readiness and deload may reduce sets; they do not invent an RIR for a quality item.
