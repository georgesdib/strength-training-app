# Spinal load and load-weighting — focused evidence review

**Date:** 2026-10-23  
**Question:** Should spinal_cost remain a load-blind ordinal, or can load/RIR/set count be
weighted into the weekly spinal cap?  
**Decision:** Keep the ordinal cap. Do **not** add load weighting. Treat any load/RIR exposure
as a separate advisory if we build one later.

---

## 1. What was reviewed

A focused, non-systematic search of biomechanics, disc-loading and injury/load-management
literature on the deadlift and hinge variants. The aim was not to derive a new equation; it was
to decide whether the current ordinal is defensible and whether a load-weighted cost is
supported.

---

## 2. What the evidence shows

### Load changes spinal kinematics and disc stress

- At 60–90% 1RM, lower-thoracic/upper-lumbar flexion and posterior pelvic tilt increased with
  load in 13 lifters. The authors conclude that high-load flexion may increase stress on the
  lumbar discs ([Shoji 2025](https://doi.org/10.3389/fspor.2025.1682991)).
- A 70% 1RM deadlift protocol (6 reps × 5 sets) acutely reduced lumbar disc apparent diffusion
  coefficient, with the largest change at L5/S1; this is consistent with mechanical stress, not
  a direct injury measure ([Yanagisawa 2020](https://doi.org/10.2463/mrms.mp.2020-0052)).

**Implication:** load matters to tissue loading. It does not follow that each kilogram or RIR
has a known, linear contribution to a weekly cap.

### Variant ordering is supported, but only ordinally

- At matched submaximal loads, the hexagonal/trap bar produced lower peak lumbar, hip and ankle
  moments than the straight bar, with a higher knee moment ([Swinton 2011](https://doi.org/10.1519/JSC.0b013e3181e73f87)).
- At national powerlifting loads, sumo reduced L4/L5 joint moment by about 10% and shear force by
  about 8% versus conventional ([Cholewicki 1991](https://doi.org/10.1249/00005768-199110000-00012)).

**Implication:** trap bar < sumo < conventional is a reasonable ordinal. There is still no
direct sumo-vs-trap-bar lumbar comparison, so 2.5 remains an assumption.

### Injury evidence does not provide a load–risk curve

- Powerlifting injury reviews describe the squat, bench and deadlift as high-demand lifts and
  discuss heavy loads, range of motion, rest and technique, but do not establish a per-kilogram
  or per-RIR risk function ([Bengtsson 2018](https://doi.org/10.1136/bmjsem-2018-000382);
  [Keogh 2016](https://doi.org/10.1007/s40279-016-0575-0)).
- A narrative review of deadlift injury prevention concludes that **abrupt increases in training
  load** are consistently associated with injury, while isolated deviations from “neutral”
  posture have unclear prospective risk. It explicitly describes the evidence as probabilistic
  and context-dependent ([Cherni 2026](https://doi.org/10.3390/sports14040151)).
- Deadlift-inclusive programmes can improve pain/function in low back pain, but were not more
  effective than low-load motor-control exercise in the reviewed studies; the evidence base is
  small ([Fischer 2021](https://doi.org/10.1123/jsr.2020-0324)).

**Implication:** the best-supported load principle is **progressive load management and avoiding
abrupt spikes**, not an exact weighted score for a single session.

---

## 3. Why the current cap stays ordinal

1. **Evidence supports ranking, not scaling.** We can defend trap bar < sumo < conventional; we
   cannot defend “cost 2.37 at 82% 1RM”.
2. **No validated load-dose model.** There is no study that maps kg, %1RM, RIR and sets onto a
   weekly spinal-risk unit.
3. **The cap is a stacking guard, not a tissue-force estimate.** Its job is to stop repeated
   high-fatigue hinge/erector exposures; readiness, RIR rules and the injury signal handle daily
   load.
4. **Set-count weighting was already rejected.** Counting per set makes a 12-set trap-bar session
   equal twelve exposures and fires the cap every week (Q8).
5. **Adding load would double-count.** Volume, readiness and RIR already respond to load. A
   second load penalty inside the cap would be difficult to interpret and easy to overfit.

The ordinal is not “evidence-precise”. It is a transparent, conservative prior with documented
assumptions (ASSUMPTION-02, Q8, docs/SPINAL-COST.md).

---

## 4. What would justify changing it

A future load-weighted model would need all of:

- a direct comparison of hinge variants at matched **relative** loads;
- a validated mapping from load/RIR/sets to a meaningful exposure unit;
- prospective or N-of-1 symptom data that distinguishes a light from a heavy exposure;
- a decision on whether the cap is a safety limit or only a pattern advisory;
- a pre-declared validation plan, not a post-hoc fit.

Until then, a load-weighted cost would be fabricated precision.

---

## 5. Recommendation

- **Keep the weekly cap as the current ordinal.**
- **Do not add load/RIR/set weighting.**
- If visibility is wanted, add a **separate advisory** such as “heavy spinal exposures this
  week” (e.g. sets at high relative intensity), clearly labelled as observational and not used
  for a pass/fail rule.
- Keep the existing assumption and open-question references visible.

## Update 2026-10-27 — policy bands, not calibration

The cap now applies bounded set-count and mean-%e1RM factors (0.85/1.00/1.15 bands) to the
ordinal base cost. This is an explicit **policy choice** recorded in `LIM-14`, not a validation
of a load–risk equation. The evidence still does not support a per-kilogram or per-set
coefficient; the factors preserve the existing 4–5 set / 75–85% baseline and make large set or
intensity deviations visible. The cap remains configurable and the ordinal ranking remains the
authoritative part of the model.
